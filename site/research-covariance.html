I

Abstract

Trust the right sensor at the right moment.

An Extended Kalman Filter is only as good as the covariances it is given. Most ROS users type them in by hand and never change them. This paper lets a model predict them while the robot drives.

Localisation robustness in dynamic conditions remains a critical challenge for ROS 2 systems, where sensor fusion is pivotal. This study evaluates an AI-driven approach that adjusts covariance parameters dynamically for pose estimation on a differential-drive robot. A regression model was integrated with the robot_localization package to adapt the EKF covariance in real time, and runs compared AI-predicted dynamic covariance against a static covariance baseline across Static, Moderate and Aggressive motion.

The AI-enabled system reached a Mean Absolute Error of 0.0061 for pose estimation and reduced median yaw prediction errors to 0.0362 rad (static) and 0.0381 rad (moderate). Aggressive motion was harder: errors reached up to 0.9491 rad, traced to data distribution bias and Random Forest limits. Dataset augmentation, LSTM modelling and online learning are proposed to close the gap, and datalogging already enables iterative re-training.

Hybrid, not end-to-end.

The paper positions itself firmly in the hybrid camp: AI is used to augment a classical EKF, not to replace it, so the filter stays interpretable, physically grounded and compatible with standard ROS 2 tooling, while running on a Raspberry Pi.

IV

Method

A student-built robot, a small forest.

A.AMR2AX: designed and built with students

The experiments ran on AMR2AX, a differential-drive robot designed by the author with a team of students through iterative CAD-to-prototype cycles, following New Product Development principles and project-based pedagogy. The chassis is aluminium U-channel, so builds repeat and can be reconfigured (omni-wheels, casters) to study how the chassis affects odometry.

A Raspberry Pi 4 (4 GB) runs Ubuntu 22.04 and ROS 2 Humble for fusion, AI inference and navigation. An ESP32 NodeMCU-32S drives two NeveRest 40 gearmotors through a Pololu VNH5019 shield and streams encoder data over a custom full-duplex serial bridge (ROS_ESP32_Bridge). Sensors: a BNO-055 IMU, an LDROBOT STL-19 2D LiDAR and a Logitech C922 camera. ros2_control's diff_drive_controller and robot_localization's EKF complete the baseline stack.

B.Why fixed covariances break

The baseline EKF trusts odometry for linear motion and yaw drift (in differential mode) and the IMU for rotation. Those fixed values fail exactly when it matters: motor slip under acceleration, irregular floors, and sensor noise that rises with heat or power fluctuations. On student-built robots, where sensor quality and mechanical tolerances vary unit to unit, hand-tuned values age badly.

The model learns the EKF's error instead. Training labels are computed by comparing filtered output with raw odometry: error_x, error_y and error_yaw. Fifteen input features cover accelerations, gyro yaw rate, odometry and commanded velocities, yaw estimates and their difference, two interaction terms and two temporal deltas.

D.Closing the loop in ROS 2

  1. Subscribe/odom, /bno055/imu_raw and /cmd_vel with sensor-data QoS.
  2. PredictEvery 0.2 s, 15 features in, error_x, error_y, error_yaw out, on /ai_tools/covariance_prediction.
  3. UpdateThe updater squares each error into a covariance and sets the EKF's 6 × 6 matrix (diagonal 0, 7, 35) through /ekf_node/set_parameters.
  4. LogEvery cycle goes to a timestamped CSV, AI on or off, feeding the next training round.

A single enable_ai launch parameter switches the whole package: with AI off, the node publishes zero errors and the updater leaves the EKF on its defaults, so the robot keeps navigating without the AI overhead.

V

Results

Strong when steady. Honest when turning hard.

A.Training: data that keeps growing

The first dataset came from teleoperation: over 16,000 records of forward and backward driving, turns and mixed trajectories with acceleration changes, recorded with ros2 bag. Every later run logged itself through the AI node, and merging those logs grew the set past 21,000 samples by version 7, split 90:10 for training and testing.

On the held-out test set the Random Forest reached MAE 0.0061 and R² 0.989 overall (0.98, 0.97 and 0.99 for x, y and yaw), with residuals tightly centred on zero.

B.On the robot: three motion regimes

The robot followed a similar Nav2 path between points 1 → 2 → 3 → 4 in the same lab, once with AI-predicted covariance and once with static covariance, under the same light and floor. Data were split by commanded angular velocity: Static (|ω| ≤ 0.01 rad/s), Moderate (up to 0.7 rad/s) and Aggressive. No sample exceeded 0.7 rad/s (the peak was ±0.667), so the paper reports Aggressive as |ω| > 0.5 rad/s, consistent with its earlier analyses. Outliers above 1.5 rad were removed.

On the Pi 4 the AI-enabled stack ran at up to 20 Hz (50 Hz baseline), stable at 5–10 Hz, averaging 25% CPU (40% peak) and about 350 MB of memory.

VI

Limitations and future work

The hard turn is the open problem.

Limitations, as the paper states them

  • Aggressive rotation. Yaw prediction error reached 0.9491 rad; mean 0.2257 rad (σ 0.2600) in the aggressive band.
  • Training-data bias. The initial dataset was mostly stable and moderate motion, with few high-dynamic transitions.
  • Random Forest extrapolation is limited under extreme inputs it has not seen.
  • Inference only. The model was trained offline; no online learning ran during operation.
  • A train/runtime mismatch. Runtime smooths yaw_filtered with an EMA (α = 0.7) that the training data did not apply.
  • Mixed maxima. Max |yaw_diff| was 0.4943 rad with AI vs 0.5771 rad without, but the filtered ROS-log peak with AI (0.689 rad) exceeds the baseline's raw yaw_diff; no significance test was run yet.

Future work

  • More aggressive data: augment the dataset with rapid rotational sequences.
  • LSTM temporal models on sliding windows, quantised to stay inside ROS 2's 50–100 Hz budget on the Pi 4.
  • Visual features from the RGB camera for spatial context.
  • Online, continual or federated learning across robots, built on the existing datalogging.
  • Hybrid switching between AI and static fallback settings, with dynamic thresholds, to avoid estimation jumps.
  • Statistics: a Wilcoxon signed-rank test to formally validate the static and moderate improvements, and industrial deployments with variable loads and uneven floors.

Where it led: the 2026 route-planning paper cites this work as the author-controlled, full-stack ROS 2 baseline previously validated on real robots that its Xplorer platforms build on, with AMCL plus an EKF (robot_localization) at 20 Hz under the semantic layers.
